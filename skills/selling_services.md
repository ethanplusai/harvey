# Selling Services

Used when `product.offering` is `services`: the operator sells work it does
for each client (software, AI or product development, consulting, accounting
or tax, design or research, legal, an agency, staffing or managed services).
The industry play for the prospect's industry, when there is one, says what
this firm offers them; this file says how to sell expertise to anyone.
Everything here sits under the strict email rules: those still win.

## What a services buyer is actually buying

Nobody wants hours. They want a result, less risk, or time back: a process
that stops breaking, a launch that ships, a close that finishes on day five,
an audit that passes, a backlog that clears. And they are buying people they
can't fully evaluate in advance, so trust does more of the work than features
do in a product sale.

- Write to one outcome for one kind of company, not a menu of capabilities.
- The firm's size, years in business and "end-to-end" scope are not reasons
  to reply. A specific result for someone like them is.
- In 2026 every buyer asks, out loud or not: "can't we do this ourselves now,
  with AI tools?" Sometimes the honest answer is yes. When it isn't, the reason
  is usually judgement, accountability, integration with how they actually
  work, or the time their own people don't have. Say which one, briefly.

## Why now: what a reason to write looks like

A services firm earns a reply when something just changed for the prospect.
Use only events the FACTS show, with their date. These are the families worth
recognising:

- **Capital**: a funding round, an acquisition (made or received), a new
  credit facility, an IPO filing. New money and new owners come with new
  expectations and a deadline.
- **People**: a new CEO, CFO, CTO, COO or head of a function. New leaders
  review vendors and processes in their first months. A key departure leaves a
  gap someone has to cover.
- **Growth**: a new office, market or country, a product line, a large
  customer win, a partnership. Growth breaks the processes that worked at the
  previous size.
- **Pressure**: a regulation reaching their sector, a certification or audit
  they announced (SOC 2, ISO 27001, HIPAA, PCI DSS), a public incident, their
  peak season (tax season for accounting firms, renewals for insurers, the
  holidays for retail).
- **Change**: a system migration, a vendor being retired, a merger to
  integrate, a re-platform. Change projects need people who have done it
  before.
- **Strain**: many open roles of the same kind (manual volume nobody can
  hire fast enough), roles open for months, a team visibly stretched.

How to use one:

- Name the event the way their own announcement or job board does, with its
  timing ("your Series B in March", "the claims roles on your board"). Never
  call it recent when the facts give no date.
- Connect it to one consequence a firm like this one fixes. Don't explain
  their business to them.
- No congratulations as an opener. Every vendor sends those that week.
- An event is a reason to talk, not proof they have the problem. Ask; don't
  diagnose.

## Proof, honestly

- Use only the proof written in the industry play or the product knowledge:
  named clients, case studies, results, certifications. If there is none, say
  what you would do first instead of inventing who you did it for.
- A relevant detail beats a big logo: "a 40-person broker" lands better with
  a 40-person broker than "a Fortune 500 insurer".
- Never imply a client relationship, a partnership, or an endorsement the
  facts don't state. Never name a client in a way their contract may not allow;
  the play says what is public.

## The engagement ladder

Services buyers rarely say yes to the whole thing first. Offer the next rung,
not the top one:

1. **A diagnostic**: a short, fixed-scope look at one problem, with a written
   result (a teardown, an assessment, a plan). Low risk for them, real signal
   for both sides. Paid or free is the operator's call; the play or the offer
   config says which. Never offer free delivery work.
2. **A pilot or first phase**: one workflow, one team, one integration, with an
   end date and a measure of success agreed up front.
3. **A project**: the full scope, once the pilot showed it works.
4. **A retainer, managed service or embedded team**: ongoing capacity, once
   they trust the delivery.

A first email asks for a reply, not a meeting. The first reply offers rung 1
or a short call, whichever the offer config prefers.

## Price and commercial shape

- Expect "what does this cost?" early. Answer with a range and what it
  depends on (scope, team size, integrations, timeline), then offer to narrow
  it. "It depends" with no number reads as hiding.
- Know the shapes buyers compare: fixed fee for defined scope, time and
  materials for evolving work, a monthly retainer for ongoing capacity, and
  outcome-linked fees where the result is measurable. Don't commit to one in
  email; the operator decides.
- Never quote a discount, a custom price or a deadline on the operator's
  behalf. Hand those to a conversation with the operator.

## Larger buyers: what slows the deal

- Procurement, security reviews and vendor questionnaires, a master services
  agreement (MSA) and a statement of work (SOW) per project, insurance
  certificates, and data-processing terms. Offering to send what they need for
  review is a reasonable second step; promising terms is not.
- Several people decide: the person with the problem, their boss with the
  budget, and someone checking the risk. A good reply gives the first one
  something they can forward.

## Objections every services firm hears

- **"We do this in-house."** Respect it. Ask what the team isn't getting to,
  and offer a defined piece that frees them, not a replacement.
- **"We already have a firm / an agency / a vendor."** Don't attack them. Ask
  what's working and what they wish moved faster; leave a way back.
- **"We can do this ourselves with AI now."** Agree where it's true. Point to
  the part that still needs experience, accountability or integration, if
  there is one.
- **"Too expensive."** Shrink the scope before the price: the smallest rung
  that produces a real answer.
- **"We got burned by a consultant before."** Take it seriously. Offer a short
  first phase with a clear end and a measure, so they judge the work.
- **"Send me something."** Send one short, specific thing (one page, one case,
  one plan outline), not a deck, and ask one question with it.
- **"Not now."** Ask when they plan the next budget or the next project, and
  leave it there. Harvey records it.
- **"Who owns the work?"** Say plainly what the offer says about ownership of
  code, documents, data and research. If the facts don't say, say the
  operator will confirm.

## Hard limits

- No professional verdicts from public information: no tax, accounting,
  legal, security, compliance or medical judgement about the prospect. "This
  regulation may apply to companies like yours" is the furthest it goes, and
  only when the facts support it.
- Accounting and legal firms have their own independence and solicitation
  rules. Don't promise outcomes, results in a dispute or with a regulator, or
  savings figures.
- Don't claim to have reviewed their systems, books, code, contracts or data.
  You read what they published.
- An opt-out is final, as with everyone else.
